**Problem:** A 12-table neighbourhood restaurant throws away too much food every day and wants to cut it.
**Limits I used:** No new equipment, about HKD 500 a month. I assumed you don't yet know which items are binned most.

## Obvious answers
What most AI replies would say. Listed on purpose; some may be exactly right.
1. **Keep a waste log.** Tally what goes in the bin for 1–2 weeks. Still the right call when you can't say which 3 items cause most of the waste. Posting the tally publicly is the same idea.
2. **Prep and order to a par level.** Base amounts on past sales by weekday. Still the right call when most waste is over-prepped food.
3. **Label and rotate stock (FIFO).** Use masking tape and a marker. Still the right call when waste is spoiled raw ingredients.
4. **Trim the menu and share ingredients across dishes.** Still the right call when many ingredients appear in only one dish.
5. **Offer smaller portions or half-sizes.** Still the right call when the waste is mostly food left on plates.
6. **Turn near-expiry stock into a daily special or staff meal.** Still the right call when the food is safe but won't last.
7. **Sell or donate surplus near closing.** This covers a discount, a "lucky bag" or a food-rescue charity. Still the right call when you reliably have cooked food left at close.
8. **Ask suppliers for smaller or more frequent deliveries.** Still the right call when you buy in bulk "just in case".

## Hidden assumptions
- The kitchen alone decides how much to cook, before customers order.
- Customers expect the full menu every day.
- Supplier pack sizes and delivery rhythms are fixed, so surplus is your problem alone.
- Surplus is only discovered at closing, then discounted or binned.
- Waste is a cost to measure, not something other people could absorb.

## Answers out of the box
### 1. Stretch: Reserve-a-portion
**Idea:** Pick the 2–3 batch-cooked dishes you bin most (a braise, soup or congee). Cook only a small base batch. When seating guests, staff say "only 8 of the braised pork today, shall I save you one?" Cook the next batch only when the reservations or a chalkboard count say so.
**Move used:** Give it to the opposite person. Customers' answers during service set the batch size.
**Assumption broken:** The kitchen alone decides how much to cook, before customers order.
**Nearest obvious answer:** #2, and **how this differs:** Past sales don't set the quantity. Live customer commitments do, and the count on the board nudges people to order the batch dish.
**Cheapest test (under one week):** Run it for one dish for 5 evenings. Write made, sold and binned on a paper slip at the till. Cost: about HKD 20 for a small board or paper.
**Biggest risk:** Staff forget to ask during busy service, and a run-out may disappoint regulars.

### 2. Leap: Split the case with the neighbours
**Idea:** Find the ingredient you most often throw out because it only comes in one big pack. Ask the 2 nearest eateries whether they'd split a case with you, or swap a surplus unopened item for one they have too much of. Keep it to raw, unopened ingredients.
**Move used:** Remove the biggest constraint. You can't change pack size, so you borrow someone else's demand to absorb the rest.
**Assumption broken:** Supplier pack sizes are fixed, so surplus is your problem alone.
**Nearest obvious answer:** #8, and **how this differs:** The supplier doesn't change anything. Another kitchen uses the surplus before it becomes waste.
**Cheapest test (under one week):** Ask the owners of 2 nearby eateries in person for one item, one time. Cost: HKD 0.
**Biggest risk:** Neighbours may not want the hassle, and it only works if you have neighbours who cook with similar ingredients.

### 3. Weird but testable: The no-buy day
**Idea:** One quiet weekday, order no perishables at all. Run a chalkboard menu of 3–4 dishes built only from what's in the fridge and pantry. Tell regulars in advance, and set the price on the board.
**Move used:** Add an absurd constraint. A hard buying ban forces the whole menu to follow the stock.
**Assumption broken:** Customers expect the full menu every day.
**Nearest obvious answer:** #6, and **how this differs:** A special is added on top of normal buying. Here the buying stops, so every dish has to come from existing stock.
**Cheapest test (under one week):** Do one no-buy day next quiet weekday. Compare that day's bin to a normal day and note takings. Cost: about HKD 20 for chalk or a marker.
**Biggest risk:** Regulars who come for a specific dish may skip that day. Takings could dip, and you'd need to keep basics like rice and cooking oil.

**Honesty:** these are not among the obvious answers above. That does not mean nobody has tried them.

For budget, none of these needs much money. Your HKD 500 would cover tape, markers, a board and a few containers, so you could also keep most of it.
